What the review found

measured in the code, 24 Aug 2026
26<select> dropdowns across our components
9checkboxes in our entire app
22checkboxes in one form of the app they liked
8CSS variables carrying their whole visual language

The gap was never features. It was how information gets into the app, and what the app sounds like while you do it.

A checkbox or chip is one tap. A <select> is tap → read a list → scroll → tap, rendered by whatever picker the phone’s manufacturer ships. A text field is the keyboard — which, in Devanagari, on a low-cost handset, for someone in their fifties, is where an app stops being usable and starts being work.

Our palette turned out to be Tailwind’s defaults, unchanged — #2563eb, #10b981, #ef4444, #f59e0b. The most common palette on the web. It reads as a developer tool, because it is the one every developer tool uses. Theirs was eight hand-picked warm values applied to every single control. Their app feels coherent because it is coherent — that is a pass we have to make, not a feature we can add.

The palette, and where it comes from

Not copied from their app. Drawn from the object this software replaces: a bound paper visitor register. Aged paper as the ground, the blue ruling of ledger paper as the structural line, binding-cloth red for actions, marigold for occasions.

Paper#f6f2e8page ground
Ink#232b36ballpoint blue-black
Rule#c3ccdbledger ruling
Binding#8a3b36every action
Marigold#dc9a2eoccasions only

The rule colour does real work: every section heading on this page and inside the proposed app sits on a horizontal line with its content hanging beneath — the way Devanagari hangs from the शिरोरेखा, and the way a ledger rules a heading. One device, used everywhere, meaning “a new column starts here”.

Seven changes to how it is touched

no data model is affected

1 · Forms rise from the bottom

Their form is a bottom sheet — it slides up from the thumb and is rounded at the top, exactly like WhatsApp and every Android app these volunteers already use.

Ours is centred in the middle of the screen: a desktop dialog on a phone. One CSS line, and much of why theirs feels native and ours feels like a website.

2 · Chips instead of dropdowns

Every short, stable list becomes tappable chips: what for, how many, relation, what they brought. All options are visible without touching anything, so people discover choices they would never have found inside a dropdown.

3 · The first screen answers a question

Theirs opens with counts for today, four large actions, and the day’s list — one scroll, no navigation. Ours opens with a month grid.

Theirs answers “how is today?”. Ours answers “what date is it?”

4 · Large soft tiles, not small buttons

Four big left-aligned targets with an emoji each, instead of btn-sm. Easier to hit, and — more importantly — easier to see.

5 · Emoji as the icon set

🍱 💐 🪔 🎂 🕯️ used consistently. No image files, renders on every phone, and it crosses reading speed: someone finds 🍱 faster than the word for it. We use emoji occasionally, which is worse than not at all.

6 · One geometry everywhere

Every control in their app: 11px padding, soft corners, one type size. No exceptions in the whole file. Generous radii read as friendly; small sharp controls read as technical.

7 · Never a blank screen

Their report screen opens already showing today. Their date fields arrive filled in. Nobody ever faces an empty form they must complete before seeing anything at all.

The risk, stated plainly

This is a wide change across every screen, in a codebase whose only UI tests check that elements render — not that they are visible.

That guard exists because a form once shipped with CSS classes that did not exist: every test passed and the button silently did nothing. A palette-and-layout sweep is exactly the change that can pass a green test suite and still look broken on a Redmi. It needs checking on a real phone, not a test run.

Do it whole, or not at all

These seven are not separate items competing for priority. They are one pass.

Doing three of seven is worse than doing none — a half-converted app is visibly inconsistent in a way today’s app is not.

Why this matters more than it looks

Overdue and Never contacted are accusations. Every morning, the app opens by listing what the volunteer has failed to do. The other app opens with who came to see the children.

Same query, same rows, same urgency — different sentence. Over forty days that difference decides whether the app is opened at all.

“Who came on this day last year?”

requested by the NGO · no migration needed

This is the one feature on the list they asked for themselves — and the data for it is already in the app, being thrown away.

Every visit is written down once, shown on its own day, and never surfaced again. The matcher that finds “this date in other years” already exists and is already tested — it is used for birthdays. It has simply never been pointed at past visits.

It is also the answer to a real problem: most days have nothing scheduled, so the app opens empty and there is no reason to look at it. A memory gives a quiet day something to show.

Three things it must get right

Never show an empty section. With 300 visitors over two years there is less than one visit per calendar date. Match the exact day first; if nothing, widen to a few days either side and say so — “around this time last year”.

Never say “we miss you” to someone who came last month. Check the last visit first. Under six months, the only safe message is thanks.

“Thanks for the gift” does not work yet. We do not record what anyone brings. That is the one change on the list that needs a new field — and it can never be filled in backwards. Every month it waits is a month of gift history that cannot be recovered.

What we are deliberately not copying

their app got things wrong too

Delete sitting beside Close

Five buttons in a row — WhatsApp, Card, Edit, Close, Delete — a destructive action next to a dismissal, both grey, on a phone. Our confirm dialog is safer and stays.

A sheet that fills the screen

With 22 checkboxes their form reaches 92% of the screen height, losing the whole point of a bottom sheet. Sheets only work if the form is short — ours must stay short.

Native alert() for every error

Jarring, styled by the phone manufacturer, and it blocks everything. Our toast is better and stays.

Editing that changes nothing

Their gratitude-message editor writes to a function that a later duplicate overwrites. The settings screen edits messages the app never sends.

An export that is one long line

Their CSV joins rows with a literal backslash-r instead of a line break, so the whole export opens in Excel as a single row.

Buttons that throw

Every button in their Reminders tab passes an unquoted ID and fails on click. None of this is visible in a demo — it is what “generated in a few minutes” costs.

What this does not touch

the reason to keep this app

Everything works offline

No account, no login, no network. The hosted-database idea was considered and dropped — each NGO keeps its own data and its own backup, as today.

People are still people

Matching by phone number stays. The other app makes a new row for every visit, so the same supporter appears three times and their name is the only thing joining them.

Birthdays still come back

A birthday recorded this year appears next year, and every year. In the other app it never returns — which is invisible in a demo and decisive in year two.

WhatsApp backup and sync stay

Untouched. A full backup is about half a megabyte compressed — it fits in a message, and that is what protects an NGO when a phone breaks.

No new permanent fields — except one

Everything shown above is surface. Only “what they brought” adds a field, and only if you approve it.

Nothing is deleted

Campaigns and bulk messaging stay until we know whether they are used. The test is one question to each NGO: how many have you sent since March?

Decisions needed before anything is built

  1. Does the right-hand phone feel like the app they liked?

    Yes, no, or “close, but —”. This is the whole reason the page exists. Everything below matters less than this answer.

  2. Marathi first, or English first?

    The preview puts Marathi first with English beneath. That is a real choice: it is warmer for field volunteers and slower for the coordinators on laptops. Ask both groups separately — they will not agree.

  3. Binding red, or something else?

    The action colour is drawn from a register’s cloth binding. It is warm and calm, but red carries meaning. If it reads wrong, marigold or a deep green work equally well and cost one line to change.

  4. Approve “what they brought”?

    The only permanent change on the list. It makes the reports genuinely useful and turns next year’s memory into “thank you for the meal you brought”. It also cannot be undone, and it cannot be backfilled.

  5. Who is the pilot?

    One phone and one laptop, upgraded first, used for two days before anyone else sees it. This is a wide visual change, and a real phone is the only test that counts.
